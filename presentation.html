3-Band FIR Filter Bank (Windowed-Sinc)

🎛️ What does it do? — 3-Band FIR Equalizer

Splits the audio into Low (<300 Hz), Mid (300–3000 Hz) and High (>3000 Hz), scales each band by its own gain, then adds the bands back together.

1
Design — firwin makes a 101-tap low-pass, band-pass and high-pass filter.
2
Filter — np.convolve(x, h, mode="same") once per band.
3
Recombine — normalize(gL·lo + gM·mid + gH·hi) — the sliders are just these three gains.
📐 Linear-Phase Band Decomposition

Decomposes audio into three non-overlapping contiguous frequency bands via FIR convolution: Bass (0–300 Hz lowpass), Mid (300–3000 Hz bandpass), and Treble (3000–8000 Hz highpass). Each sub-band is multiplied by its linear gain, then summed back together.

9-Band IIR Peaking Cascade (RBJ Audio EQ Cookbook)

🎚️ What does it do? — IIR Peaking Cascade

Every one of the 9 ISO bands (60 Hz–16 kHz) gets one RBJ peaking biquad (Q = 1.4) whose centre gain is set in dB. The biquads run one after another.

1
Coefficients — A = 10^(dB/40), α = sin(ω₀)/2Q give b₀..b₂ and a₀..a₂.
2
Skip flat bands — bands with |gain| < 1e-6 are left out — zero cost.
3
Cascade — signal.lfilter per biquad; rescale only if peak > 1.

Equalizer · Architecture 3 of 3

FIR Windowed-Sinc Bands: Strictly Linear Phase

📐 What does it do? — FIR Windowed-Sinc Bands

Cuts the spectrum into 9 bands at the geometric-mean crossovers √(fi·fi+1) using 201-tap linear-phase FIR filters, then sums the scaled bands in parallel.

1
Crossovers — _band_edges() puts each split halfway (in log-frequency) between neighbours.
2
Filters — first band low-pass, last band high-pass, the 7 between are band-pass.
3
Parallel sum — gain = 10^(dB/20); Σ gain·convolve(x, h), then normalize.
✨ Why Linear Phase Matters in Studio Mastering

Standard IIR biquad equalizers introduce non-uniform phase delays: different frequency components are delayed by different amounts. This causes transient smearing — drum kicks lose punch and acoustic guitars lose snap.

IIR Biquad Phase Distortion
Non-linear phase response shifts high and low frequencies relative to each other, dispersing sharp transient attacks into bloated waveforms.
FIR Strict Phase Linearity (Constant Group Delay)
Because impulse response is symmetric (h[n] = h[N - 1 - n]), every frequency is delayed by exactly τ_g = (N - 1) / 2 samples. Zero waveform distortion!

Equalizer · Hybrid Design

Shelving + Peaking Hybrid (Musical Hi-Fi EQ)

🏔️ What does it do? — Shelving + Peaking Hybrid

Same 9 gains, but the two edge bands become true shelves: 60 Hz is a low-shelf and 16 kHz a high-shelf (Q = 0.707), so boosts stay flat out to DC and Nyquist. Bands 2–8 stay peaking.

1
Band 1 — _low_shelf_biquad lifts everything below f₀.
2
Bands 2–8 — _peaking_biquad (Q = 1.4) — identical to the IIR cascade.
3
Band 9 — _high_shelf_biquad lifts everything above f₀.
🎧 Musical Shelf vs Peaking Biquads

Pure peaking filters drop back to 0 dB at extreme ends. The Shelving Hybrid replaces the 60 Hz filter with a Low-Shelf biquad (boosting deep sub-bass into a flat warm plateau) and the 16 kHz filter with a High-Shelf biquad (adding open acoustic air), while keeping 7 surgical peaking filters in between.

3 Envelope Extraction Demodulators Compared

〰️ What does it do? — three envelope detectors

After band-passing around the tone, one of three methods turns the wave into a smooth 0–1 envelope that is thresholded into ON/OFF keying.

1
Hilbert — |hilbert(x)| — exact instantaneous amplitude.
2
Rectify + LPF — |x| then 2nd-order 30 Hz Butterworth filtfilt.
3
Short-time RMS — 10 ms moving-average of x², square-rooted.
🔬 Demodulation Topologies
1. Hilbert Analytic Signal (|x + j·x̂|)
Instantaneous complex envelope with zero phase lag. Extremely accurate on clean signals.
2. Rectify + 30 Hz Butterworth LPF
Classic AM diode envelope detector. Full-wave absolute value followed by 2nd-order IIR smoothing.
3. Short-Time RMS Energy Window
Moving average convolution of squared samples: √(x² * h). Immune to noise bursts!

Method 3 — Spectral Subtraction Denoise
The idea in one line: "Estimate what the noise looks like, then subtract it from every piece of the signal."
📌 Before we start — What is STFT?

A normal FFT takes the entire signal and converts it to frequencies — but that gives you no time information. You know which frequencies exist, but not when they appear.

STFT (Short-Time Fourier Transform) fixes this. It chops the signal into small overlapping chunks called frames, and runs FFT on each frame separately. So you get a 2D map: frequency vs. time — this is called a spectrogram.

In our code: each frame is 512 samples long, and neighboring frames overlap by 256 samples (50%). This overlap makes the final output smooth when we stitch frames back together.
📌 Why do we split into Magnitude and Phase?

When you do FFT on a frame, each frequency bin gives you a complex number. A complex number has two parts:

  • Magnitude |Zxx| = how loud that frequency is (the energy / amplitude)
  • Phase ∠Zxx = where in the wave cycle that frequency is at that moment (timing info)

Noise mainly affects the magnitude (it adds extra energy). The phase is mostly fine. So our strategy is: clean up the magnitude, keep the phase untouched, recombine them.

📌 The Key Assumption

This method assumes the first few frames of the recording are noise-only (the speaker hasn't started talking yet — just background noise). We use those frames to learn what noise "looks like" in the frequency domain, so we can subtract it from the rest.

In our code: noise_frames = 10 → we use the first 10 STFT frames as the "noise-only" reference.

Spectral Subtraction — Step-by-Step Code Walkthrough
1
Set up STFT parameters
nperseg = 512 noverlap = nperseg // 2 # = 256

nperseg = 512 means each frame will contain 512 audio samples. At our sample rate (44100 Hz), that's about 11.6 milliseconds of audio per frame.

noverlap = 256 means neighboring frames share 256 samples (50% overlap). This overlap is important — without it, the reconstructed signal would have audible clicks at frame boundaries.

▼
2
Compute the STFT → get the spectrogram
f, t, Zxx = signal.stft(x, fs=fs, nperseg=nperseg, noverlap=noverlap)

This one line does all the heavy lifting. signal.stft() from SciPy:

  • Chops the signal x into overlapping frames of 512 samples each
  • Applies a window function (Hann window by default) to each frame to smooth the edges
  • Runs FFT on each windowed frame
  • Returns a 2D complex matrix Zxx with shape (257 frequency bins × number of frames)

It also returns f (the frequency values for each row) and t (the time center of each frame).

▼
3
Separate magnitude and phase
mag = np.abs(Zxx) # magnitude of each complex number phase = np.angle(Zxx) # phase angle of each complex number

Each entry in Zxx is a complex number like a + bj. We split it:

  • mag = √(a² + b²) → tells us the energy at that frequency in that frame
  • phase = arctan(b/a) → tells us the timing/position of the wave

We will only modify mag. The phase is saved and reused unchanged at the end.

▼
4
Estimate the noise power from the first 10 frames
noise_power = np.mean(mag[:, :10] ** 2, axis=1, keepdims=True)

Let's break this down piece by piece:

  • mag[:, :10] → take ALL frequency bins (rows), but only the first 10 frames (columns). These frames are assumed to be noise-only.
  • ** 2 → square the magnitudes to get power (energy). Power is magnitude squared.
  • np.mean(..., axis=1) → average across those 10 frames for each frequency bin separately. So you get one average noise power value per frequency.
  • keepdims=True → keeps the result as a column vector so it can be broadcast (subtracted from) the full 2D matrix later.
Result: noise_power is a column vector with shape (257 × 1). Each value tells us: "on average, how much power does the noise have at this particular frequency?"
▼
5
Subtract noise power from every frame (the core operation)
clean_power = mag ** 2 - alpha * noise_power # alpha = 2.0 clean_power = np.maximum(clean_power, beta * noise_power) # beta = 0.02 clean_mag = np.sqrt(clean_power)

Line 1: For every frequency bin in every frame, subtract the estimated noise power. We multiply noise by alpha = 2.0 (over-subtraction factor) — we intentionally subtract more noise than we estimated to be aggressive about cleaning.

Line 2: After subtraction, some values might go negative (we subtracted too much). Negative power doesn't make physical sense. But instead of setting them to zero, we set them to a small floor: beta × noise_power where beta = 0.02. This is called the spectral floor.

Why not just zero? Setting to exactly zero creates an artifact called "musical noise" — random twinkling tones that sound like a broken music box. The small floor (β) keeps a tiny hiss which sounds much more natural than those artifacts.

Line 3: Take the square root to convert power back to magnitude. Now clean_mag is our cleaned-up magnitude spectrogram.

▼
6
Reconstruct the complex spectrogram and convert back to audio
clean_Zxx = clean_mag * np.exp(1j * phase) _, clean_signal = signal.istft(clean_Zxx, fs=fs, nperseg=nperseg, noverlap=noverlap)

Line 1: We need to turn magnitude + phase back into a complex number. The formula is: Z = magnitude × e^(j × phase). This is Euler's formula — it rebuilds the complex spectrogram using our cleaned magnitude and the original phase.

Line 2: signal.istft() is the Inverse STFT. It takes each frame's FFT, converts it back to time-domain samples, then overlap-adds all frames together to reconstruct one continuous audio signal.

▼
7
Match the output length to the input
if len(clean_signal) > len(x): clean_signal = clean_signal[:len(x)] # trim extra elif len(clean_signal) < len(x): clean_signal = np.pad(clean_signal, (0, len(x) - len(clean_signal))) # pad with zeros

STFT + ISTFT sometimes produces an output that is slightly longer or shorter than the original (due to framing and zero-padding). This safety check trims or pads so the clean output is exactly the same length as the input. Without this, the rest of the app would crash when trying to compare or play signals of different lengths.

✅ Summary of the whole flow

Signal → chop into frames (STFT) → separate magnitude & phase → estimate noise from first 10 frames → subtract noise power from all frames (with floor to avoid artifacts) → recombine cleaned magnitude + original phase → stitch frames back into audio (ISTFT) → match length → done.

📌 How is this different from Spectral Subtraction?

Both methods start the same way: STFT → magnitude/phase → noise estimate from first frames.

The difference is what happens next:

  • Spectral Subtraction does: clean = signal_power − noise_power (subtraction)
  • Wiener Filter does: clean = signal × gain, where gain = 1 − noise/signal (multiplication by a smart ratio)

The Wiener approach is smoother — instead of crudely subtracting, it gently scales each frequency up or down based on how much noise is present at that specific frequency. Think of it like a dimmer switch vs. an on/off switch.

📌 What is the "Wiener Gain"?

The gain is a number between 0 and 1 calculated for every single frequency bin in every single frame:

gain = max( 1 − (noise_power / signal_power) , 0 )
  • If a frequency bin has lots of signal and little noise → noise/signal is small → gain ≈ 1.0 → we keep almost everything.
  • If a frequency bin has mostly noise and little signal → noise/signal is large (close to 1 or more) → gain ≈ 0.0 → we suppress it almost completely.
  • If noise_power > signal_power, the formula would give a negative number, so we clamp it to 0 with max(..., 0).

This is optimal in the least-squares sense — it minimizes the expected squared error between the true clean signal and our estimate. That's why Wiener filter is considered the "smartest" of the four methods.

📌 Why does the code add 1e-10?

You'll see signal_power + 1e-10 in the division. This is a tiny number (0.0000000001) added to prevent division by zero. If any frequency bin has exactly zero signal power, dividing by it would crash the program or produce infinity. Adding this tiny epsilon makes it safe without changing the result in any meaningful way.

Identical to Spectral Subtraction. We chop the signal into overlapping 512-sample frames and run FFT on each frame. Zxx is the resulting 2D complex spectrogram.

▼
2
Separate magnitude and phase
mag = np.abs(Zxx) phase = np.angle(Zxx)

Same as before. mag = how loud each frequency is in each frame. phase = timing information. We will modify mag and keep phase unchanged.

▼
3
Estimate noise power from the first 10 frames
noise_power = np.mean(mag[:, :10] ** 2, axis=1, keepdims=True)

Again identical to Spectral Subtraction. Take the first 10 frames (assumed noise-only), square the magnitudes to get power, and average across those 10 frames for each frequency bin. Result: a column vector of shape (257 × 1) — one noise power estimate per frequency.

▼
4
Calculate the signal power for every bin in every frame
signal_power = mag ** 2

This is the observed power at every frequency bin in every frame. Note: this includes both signal AND noise mixed together. It's not pure signal power — but we use it as the denominator in the gain formula. The idea is: if the total observed power is much larger than the noise estimate, then most of it must be real signal.

▼
5
⭐ Calculate the Wiener gain (the heart of the algorithm)
gain = np.maximum(1.0 - noise_power / (signal_power + 1e-10), 0.0)

This is the entire intelligence of the Wiener filter in one line. Let's trace through different scenarios:

Scenario A — Strong signal, weak noise:
signal_power = 100, noise_power = 5
gain = 1.0 − 5/100 = 1.0 − 0.05 = 0.95
→ We keep 95% of this frequency. Almost all of it is real signal. ✓
Scenario B — Medium signal, medium noise:
signal_power = 20, noise_power = 10
gain = 1.0 − 10/20 = 1.0 − 0.5 = 0.5
→ We keep 50%. Half of it is noise, half is signal — we reduce it to half. ⚠️
Scenario C — Weak signal, strong noise:
signal_power = 3, noise_power = 10
gain = 1.0 − 10/3 = 1.0 − 3.33 = −2.33 → clamped to 0.0
→ We suppress it completely. It's mostly noise anyway. ✗

The + 1e-10 in the denominator prevents division by zero when signal_power is exactly 0.

▼
6
Apply the gain to the magnitude
clean_mag = mag * gain

Multiply each magnitude value by its corresponding gain. Where gain is close to 1, the magnitude is barely changed (signal-dominant bins). Where gain is close to 0, the magnitude is nearly zeroed out (noise-dominant bins). This is the actual noise removal happening.

Notice: Unlike Spectral Subtraction, there is no spectral floor (β) needed. Because we're multiplying (not subtracting), the output smoothly transitions between "keep" and "suppress" — there are no sudden jumps that would cause musical noise artifacts.
▼
7
Reconstruct complex spectrogram → ISTFT → match length
clean_Zxx = clean_mag * np.exp(1j * phase) _, clean_signal = signal.istft(clean_Zxx, fs=fs, nperseg=nperseg, noverlap=noverlap) # Match original length if len(clean_signal) > len(x): clean_signal = clean_signal[:len(x)] elif len(clean_signal) < len(x): clean_signal = np.pad(clean_signal, (0, len(x) - len(clean_signal)))

Exactly the same as Spectral Subtraction:

  1. Recombine cleaned magnitude + original phase into complex numbers using Euler's formula
  2. Run Inverse STFT to convert frames back into a continuous audio signal
  3. Trim or pad to match the original input length exactly
✅ Summary — How Wiener compares to Spectral Subtraction
Steps 1–3 Identical. Both do STFT → separate mag/phase → estimate noise from first 10 frames.
Step 4 (Key Difference) Spectral Sub subtracts noise power.
Wiener multiplies by a gain ratio (0 to 1).
Spectral floor? Spectral Sub needs β to prevent artifacts.
Wiener doesn't need it — gain naturally stays ≥ 0.
Parameters Spectral Sub needs tuning: α (how aggressively to subtract), β (floor level).
Wiener: just noise_frames. The gain self-adapts.
Steps 5–7 Identical. Both recombine with original phase → ISTFT → match length.
🧠 Why is Wiener "optimal"?

The Wiener gain formula G = 1 − N/S is mathematically derived to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) between the true clean signal and our estimate. No other linear filter can do better than this — that's why it's called the optimal linear estimator. Spectral Subtraction is a good heuristic, but Wiener is the theoretically best answer to the question: "given what I know about the noise, what's my best guess of the clean signal?"

📌 What does it do?

Trim cuts out a section of audio. You give it a start time and an end time (in seconds), and it returns only the audio between those two points. Everything before the start and after the end is thrown away.

For example: you have a 10-second recording, but only the part from 2s to 6s is useful. Trim gives you just that 4-second piece.

if end_idx <= start_idx: return x[:1].copy() # return just 1 sample if range is invalid

If the user accidentally gives a start time after the end time, we can't trim anything useful. Instead of crashing, we return a tiny 1-sample array.

NumPy array slicing — takes only the samples between start and end indices. .copy() makes a fresh copy so the original audio data is not affected.

Feature 3 of 7
Reverse
📌 What does it do?

Reverse flips the audio backwards. The last sample becomes the first, and the first becomes the last. It sounds like playing a tape in reverse — words become unrecognizable, cymbal crashes turn into swells, etc.

🔍 Step-by-step Code Walkthrough
def reverse(x): return x[::-1].copy()

This is literally one line of actual logic. Let's understand it:

  • x[::-1] — This is Python slice notation with a step of -1, which means "read the array from end to start". It's the standard Python idiom for reversing any sequence.
  • .copy() — Makes a new independent copy. Without this, the reversed array would still be linked to the original data in memory, and modifying one could affect the other.
Example: If the original signal is [1, 5, 3, 8, 2], the reversed signal is [2, 8, 3, 5, 1].
💡 DSP Concept: Time Reversal
In DSP terms, this is time reversal: y[n] = x[N-1-n]. It mirrors the signal across the time axis. The frequency content stays the same (same pitches), but the phase is flipped — which is why it sounds "backwards" even though the frequencies haven't changed.
⚠️
Before Time Scale · Concept
Why Not Just Resample?
📌 The problem with naive speed change

The simplest way to change speed is to just read the samples faster or slower — like playing a tape at a different motor speed. But that also changes the pitch: speed it up and voices sound like chipmunks; slow it down and everything sounds deep and dragging.

That's because pitch is just how many wave cycles happen per second. Naive resampling squeezes or stretches the whole waveform — cycles and all — so speed and pitch get stuck moving together.

💡 The fix: decouple time from pitch
What we actually want is to change how long the audio takes to play without changing how many times per second the waveform oscillates. Simple resampling can't do that — it has only one knob, and it controls both at once. WSOLA (Waveform-Similarity Overlap-Add) gives us a second knob: it re-assembles small overlapping frames of the original waveform at a new spacing, so the output plays faster or slower while each frame's internal wave shape — and therefore its pitch — stays untouched. That's exactly what the next page implements, step by step.
⏩
Feature 4 of 7
Time Scale (Speed Change)
📌 What does it do?

Changes the playback speed without changing the pitch. Speed > 1.0 makes audio faster (shorter), speed < 1.0 makes it slower (longer). Unlike simply re-sampling (which would make the voice sound chipmunk-like or deep), this keeps the voice sounding natural.

🔍 Step-by-step Code Walkthrough (WSOLA)
1
Clamp speed & check for no change
speed = max(0.25, min(speed, 4.0)) # limit: 0.25x to 4x if abs(speed - 1.0) < 0.01: return x.copy() # speed ≈ 1.0, no change needed

Speed is limited between 0.25× (4 times slower) and 4× (4 times faster). If speed is basically 1.0 (within 0.01), just return the original — no processing needed.

▼
2
Set up frame, hop & search-tolerance parameters
frame_len = int(0.050 * fs) # 50ms analysis frames hop_syn = frame_len // 2 # synthesis hop (50% overlap, FIXED) hop_ana = max(1, int(hop_syn * speed)) # analysis hop, scaled by speed tolerance = frame_len // 4 # WSOLA search radius (samples) window = np.hanning(frame_len)

Unlike a naive OLA, here it's hop_syn that's fixed (output timing never changes) and hop_ana that scales with speed (how far we jump through the input each frame). tolerance defines how far WSOLA is allowed to nudge the read position away from that nominal jump — this is the piece plain OLA doesn't have.

▼
3
Write the first frame directly
output[:frame_len] += x[:frame_len] * window win_sum[:frame_len] += window

There's nothing to correlate against yet — no previous output tail exists — so frame 0 is just windowed and dropped straight into the output. Every later frame will be chosen by matching against what's already been written.

▼
4
Nominal position & the search window
ana_pos_nominal = int(i * hop_ana) search_lo = max(0, ana_pos_nominal - tolerance) search_hi = min(len(x) - frame_len, ana_pos_nominal + tolerance) so = i * hop_syn # fixed output position prev_tail = output[so : so + frame_len] # what we must overlap with

ana_pos_nominal is where a plain OLA would just grab the next frame from. WSOLA instead treats it as a starting guess and opens a ±tolerance search range around it. prev_tail is the already-synthesized region this new frame is about to overlap — it's the thing the search tries to match.

▼
5
Cross-correlation search — the WSOLA core
best_pos, best_corr = ana_pos_nominal, -np.inf for candidate in range(search_lo, search_hi + 1): seg = x[candidate : candidate + frame_len] corr = np.dot(prev_tail, seg) # similarity score if corr > best_corr: best_corr, best_pos = corr, candidate

Every candidate start position inside the tolerance range is tried. Whichever segment's waveform is most similar (highest dot-product) to prev_tail wins — that's the position that will overlap without a phase mismatch. This is exactly the step a plain OLA skips, and exactly why OLA can click while WSOLA doesn't.

▼
6
Overlap-add the winning frame
frame = x[best_pos : best_pos + frame_len] output[so : so + frame_len] += frame * window win_sum[so : so + frame_len] += window

The best-matching frame (not necessarily the nominal one) is windowed and added at the fixed synthesis position so. win_sum keeps a running total of window weight at every sample, needed for the next step.

▼
7
Normalize by window sum & trim
win_sum[win_sum < 1e-8] = 1e-8 # avoid divide-by-zero output /= win_sum # normalize overlapping regions return normalize(output[:last_nonzero])

Where frames overlap, samples were added multiple times — dividing by win_sum compensates so amplitude stays correct. Trailing samples with near-zero window weight (past the last real frame) are trimmed off before the final normalize().

💡 DSP Concept: WSOLA vs plain OLA
Plain OLA reads frames at a fixed offset and hopes they line up when overlapped — they often don't, causing audible clicks. WSOLA adds one idea on top: before placing each frame, search a small window around the expected read position and pick whichever segment's waveform best matches (via cross-correlation) what's already been written. Same overlap-add machinery, but the join is now waveform-continuous, which is what keeps pitch and timbre natural at any speed.

Fade In / Fade Out
📌 What does it do?

Fade-in gradually increases the volume from silence (0) to full volume (1) at the beginning. Fade-out gradually decreases from full volume to silence at the end. This prevents sudden harsh starts/stops and gives audio a smooth, professional feel.

🔍 Step-by-step Code Walkthrough
def fade(x, fade_in_s=0.0, fade_out_s=0.0, fs=FS):
1
Prepare the signal and calculate fade lengths
x = x.copy().astype(float) n_in = min(int(fade_in_s * fs), len(x) // 2) n_out = min(int(fade_out_s * fs), len(x) // 2)

Copy the signal so we don't modify the original. Convert to float for decimal multiplication. Calculate how many samples the fade covers — but cap it at half the signal length so fade-in and fade-out don't overlap.

▼
2
Apply fade-in (multiply by ramp 0 → 1)
if n_in > 0: x[:n_in] *= np.linspace(0, 1, n_in)

np.linspace(0, 1, n_in) creates a ramp: [0.0, 0.001, 0.002, ..., 0.999, 1.0]. Multiplying the first n_in samples by this ramp makes them start silent and gradually reach full volume. The first sample is multiplied by 0 (silent), the last fade-in sample by 1 (full).

▼
3
Apply fade-out (multiply by ramp 1 → 0)
if n_out > 0: x[-n_out:] *= np.linspace(1, 0, n_out)

Same idea in reverse: [1.0, 0.999, ..., 0.001, 0.0]. The last n_out samples gradually go to silence. x[-n_out:] selects the last n_out samples using negative indexing.

💡 DSP Concept: Amplitude Envelope
This is element-wise multiplication of the signal by an "envelope" function. It's the simplest form of amplitude modulation — you're shaping how loud the signal is over time without changing its frequency content. Every professional audio tool uses this technique.

Feature 6 of 7
Smooth
📌 What does it do?

Smooth softens the audio by averaging nearby samples together. It removes sharp spikes and rapid fluctuations, making the waveform gentler. Larger kernel = more smoothing = softer, more muffled sound.

This is exactly the same as the Moving Average Filter from the Noise Remover — just used as an editing effect here.

🔍 Step-by-step Code Walkthrough
def smooth(x, kernel_size=50):
1
Clamp kernel size and build the kernel
kernel_size = max(1, int(kernel_size)) # at least 1 kernel = np.ones(kernel_size) / kernel_size

The kernel is an array of equal values that sum to 1. For kernel_size = 50, it's [1/50, 1/50, 1/50, ..., 1/50] (50 values). This is a uniform averaging kernel — each nearby sample contributes equally to the average.

▼
2
Convolve the signal with the kernel
return normalize(np.convolve(x, kernel, mode='same'))

np.convolve(x, kernel, mode='same') slides the kernel across the signal. At each position, it multiplies the kernel values with the overlapping signal values and sums them up — effectively computing the average of kernel_size neighboring samples. mode='same' means the output has the same length as the input.

💡 DSP Concept: Convolution as Low-Pass Filter
Convolution is the CSE220 core primitive used here! Convolving with a uniform kernel acts as a low-pass filter — it lets slow (low frequency) changes through but removes fast (high frequency) changes. That's why the sound becomes "softer" — high frequencies are what give audio its crispness and detail.

Feature 7 of 7
Echo / Reverb
📌 What does it do?

Adds an echo effect — you hear the original sound, then a slightly quieter copy after a short delay, then an even quieter copy, and so on. It simulates the effect of sound bouncing off walls in a room or canyon.

Parameters: delay_s = 0.15 (150ms between echoes), decay = 0.5 (each echo is half as loud), n_repeats = 4 (4 echo copies).

🔍 Step-by-step Code Walkthrough
1
Build the impulse response
h_len = int(delay_s * fs * n_repeats) + 1 h = np.zeros(h_len)

Create an array of zeros long enough to hold all the echoes. This will be our impulse response — it describes the "room" or "environment" that creates the echoes.

▼
2
Place decaying spikes at regular intervals
for k in range(n_repeats + 1): # 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 idx = int(k * delay_s * fs) # position of echo k h[idx] = decay ** k # amplitude of echo k

This loop places "spikes" (impulses) in the impulse response:

  • k=0: at position 0, value 0.5⁰ = 1.0 (the original sound, full volume)
  • k=1: at position 6615 (150ms later), value 0.5¹ = 0.5 (first echo, half volume)
  • k=2: at position 13230 (300ms later), value 0.5² = 0.25 (quarter volume)
  • k=3: at position 19845 (450ms later), value 0.5³ = 0.125
  • k=4: at position 26460 (600ms later), value 0.5⁴ = 0.0625 (barely audible)
So h looks like: [1.0, 0, 0, ..., 0.5, 0, 0, ..., 0.25, 0, 0, ..., 0.125, 0, ..., 0.0625, 0, ...] — a series of spikes getting smaller and smaller.
▼
3
Convolve the signal with the impulse response
return normalize(np.convolve(x, h, mode='full')[:len(x)])

Convolution with the impulse response — this is the magic. When you convolve a signal with an impulse response, each spike in h creates a delayed, scaled copy of the original signal. The spike at position 0 (value 1.0) keeps the original. The spike at position 6615 (value 0.5) creates a half-volume copy delayed by 150ms. And so on for all echoes.

mode='full' produces a longer output (original + echo tail), but [:len(x)] trims it back to the original length. normalize() prevents clipping.

💡 DSP Concept: Convolution with Impulse Response = LTI System
This is a textbook LTI (Linear Time-Invariant) system from CSE220. The impulse response h[n] completely defines the system's behavior. Convolving any input signal with h gives the system's output. Real-world reverbs in concert halls, caves, and recording studios can all be modeled this way — record the impulse response of the room, then convolve any audio with it.

💡 Why average, not vote?
A simple mean means one algorithm's weak spot (e.g. Spectral Fingerprint on very short clips) gets balanced out by the other two, rather than any single method dominating the final ranking shown in the search results.
